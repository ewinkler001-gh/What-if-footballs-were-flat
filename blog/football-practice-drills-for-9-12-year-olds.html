Football Practice Drills for 9–12 Year Olds

Players between the ages of 9 and 12 are ready to begin developing more advanced football skills. At this age, practices can move beyond the basic fundamentals and introduce players to more challenging passing, catching, footwork, agility, and teamwork drills.

The key is to keep practices challenging without making them feel like work. Young athletes should have plenty of opportunities to compete, learn, make mistakes, and most importantly, have fun.

How Long Should a Youth Football Practice Be?

The ideal practice length depends on the age and experience of the players, but practices for 9–12 year olds should generally include a combination of warm-ups, skill development, team activities, and a short scrimmage or game.

Instead of spending too much time on one drill, rotate activities regularly. Keeping players moving helps maintain their attention and gives everyone more opportunities to participate.

1. Dynamic Warm-Up

Start practice with a dynamic warm-up rather than immediately jumping into football drills.

Players can perform:

  • Light jogging
  • High knees
  • Butt kicks
  • Side shuffles
  • Skipping
  • Short acceleration runs

A good warm-up prepares players for practice while also improving coordination and movement.

2. Partner Passing Drill

Have players pair up approximately 10 to 15 yards apart. Players throw the football back and forth while concentrating on accuracy and proper throwing mechanics.

Encourage players to:

  • Point their front shoulder toward the target.
  • Step toward the receiver.
  • Follow through toward the target.
  • Lead the receiver when appropriate.

For additional passing practice, see How to Improve Passing Accuracy in Youth Football.

3. Three-Cone Footwork Drill

Place three cones in a triangle approximately five yards apart. Players move around the cones using different footwork patterns.

Try:

  • Forward sprints
  • Backpedaling
  • Side shuffles
  • Quick changes of direction

This drill helps develop balance, agility, and body control.

4. Route Running Drill

At ages 9–12, players can begin learning basic passing routes.

Set up cones to mark the beginning and end of each route. Have receivers practice routes such as:

  • Slants
  • Out routes
  • In routes
  • Hooks
  • Go routes

Focus on sharp cuts and proper footwork rather than simply running as fast as possible.

5. Catch and Turn Drill

Have a receiver catch a short pass and immediately turn upfield.

The drill teaches players to transition quickly from catching the football to running with it.

Remind players to secure the ball before turning upfield.

For additional catching advice, read How to Catch a Football: Tips for Beginners.

6. Passing Target Drill

Set up several targets at different distances. Players attempt to hit each target with accurate passes.

You can make the drill more challenging by assigning different point values to each target.

This turns passing practice into a friendly competition while helping quarterbacks develop accuracy.

7. Agility Ladder Drill

Agility ladders are useful for developing quick feet and coordination.

Try different patterns including:

  • One foot in each square
  • Two feet in each square
  • In-and-out steps
  • Lateral movements

Keep the emphasis on controlled movements and proper technique rather than simply completing the ladder as quickly as possible.

8. Ball Security Drill

Ball security becomes increasingly important as players become more competitive.

Have players carry the football through a short obstacle course while practicing proper ball position.

Teach players to keep the football:

  • High against the chest
  • Covered with both hands when necessary
  • Away from defenders

Coaches can introduce light defensive pressure as players become more comfortable.

9. Flag Football Defensive Drill

For flag football teams, players can practice closing the distance between themselves and a ball carrier before attempting a flag pull.

Focus on:

  • Taking a good angle
  • Staying under control
  • Watching the runner's hips
  • Pulling the flag cleanly

Avoid encouraging players to make unnecessary contact.

10. Receiver Reaction Drill

Have a receiver line up several yards away from the quarterback. The quarterback can point left or right just before the receiver starts the route.

The receiver reacts to the signal and runs in that direction before receiving a pass.

This drill develops reaction time, concentration, and communication.

11. Quarterback Rollout Drill

Quarterbacks can practice moving laterally before setting their feet and throwing to a receiver.

Start slowly and emphasize balance and accuracy.

As players improve, coaches can add a simulated defender or secondary receiver to make the drill more game-like.

12. Team Passing Challenge

Divide the team into two groups and give each group a target or series of targets to hit.

Teams earn points for completing accurate passes. The first team to reach a predetermined score wins.

This combines competition with fundamental passing practice and encourages teammates to support one another.

13. Pursuit Drill

Players practice taking the proper angle toward a ball carrier.

Set up an offensive player with the football and have a defender start several yards behind and to the side.

The defender attempts to take the correct pursuit angle while the ball carrier runs toward a designated area.

For young players, focus on positioning and effort rather than hard contact.

14. Red Zone Passing Drill

Set up a small playing area and have the offense attempt to complete a pass into a designated scoring zone.

This teaches players to make quick decisions when space is limited.

It also encourages quarterbacks and receivers to communicate and recognize open space.

15. Youth Football Scrimmage

Finish practice with a short scrimmage so players can use the skills they practiced.

Keep the rules simple and rotate players through different positions when appropriate.

A scrimmage gives coaches an opportunity to see which skills need more attention at the next practice.

How to Keep 9–12 Year Olds Engaged

Players in this age group can handle more complicated drills than younger children, but practices should still move at a good pace.

  • Keep lines short.
  • Give players plenty of repetitions.
  • Use friendly competitions.
  • Explain drills clearly before starting.
  • Correct mistakes positively.
  • Rotate players through different positions.
  • Finish with a fun activity or scrimmage.

Use the Right Equipment

A few basic pieces of equipment can make youth football practices much more effective.

  • Youth-size footballs
  • Training cones
  • Agility ladder
  • Flag football belts
  • Passing targets
  • Practice jerseys or pinnies

For younger players, choosing the correct football size is especially important. See our guide to what size football kids should use.

Safety Should Always Come First

Football practices should be challenging, but safety should never be sacrificed for competition.

Make sure players have appropriate equipment, stay hydrated, and understand proper techniques. Coaches should also pay attention to signs of fatigue and allow players to take breaks when necessary.

For more information, read our Football Safety Tips for Kids.

Practice at Home

Players don't need a full football field to improve. Many of these drills can be adapted for a backyard, park, or other open space.

Even 15 to 20 minutes of throwing, catching, footwork, or agility practice can give young athletes additional repetitions between team practices.

For more ideas, visit our Backyard Football Drills for Kids article.

Final Thoughts

Football practice for 9–12 year olds should combine skill development, competition, teamwork, and fun. At this age, players are ready to begin learning more advanced techniques while continuing to strengthen the fundamentals.

The most effective youth football practices keep players moving, give them plenty of opportunities to practice, and create a positive environment where mistakes are treated as part of learning.

Whether you're a parent practicing with your child or a volunteer coach preparing for the next team practice, these drills can help young players become more confident and capable football players.
